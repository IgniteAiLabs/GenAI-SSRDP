Better prompts

Ask clearly. Learn actively.

11 questions

A prompt is the instruction or question you give an AI tool. It can include your goal, background, examples, constraints and the format you want for the answer.

Explain your task, the relevant context, the intended audience and the desired output. Add limits such as length, language or difficulty. Ask for clarification when essential information is missing.

No. A role such as “act as an English teacher” can guide the tone and structure, but it does not confer professional qualifications or guarantee correct answers.

Zero-shot prompting means asking the model to perform a task without giving examples of the desired input and output. A zero-shot prompt can still be detailed and include plenty of context.

Few-shot prompting gives the model a small number of examples showing how to perform the task. Adding a role or detailed instructions alone does not make a prompt few-shot.

Your starting point helps the assistant choose suitable vocabulary, examples and depth. “I am beginning a solar training course” gives more useful direction than “explain solar.”

06

Design Thinking

People, problems & possibilities

10 questions

Design Thinking is an approach to understanding people’s needs, defining a useful problem, exploring ideas, trying prototypes and learning from feedback. The steps often repeat as understanding improves.

Empathy helps us understand the people affected by a problem and their circumstances. It reduces the chance of building something impressive that does not meet their actual needs.

Before building, we need to understand who will use the house and how they want to live. Choices about light, heat and layout depend on the context. The same reasoning applies to training and technology projects.

A stakeholder is someone affected by or involved in a decision. Depending on the project, this may include users, workers, trainers, customers, maintainers, funders and decision-makers.

Desirable asks whether people need or want the solution. Feasible asks whether it can work in the real conditions. Viable asks whether it can be sustained with the available resources and operating model.

A participant expressed interest in a robot to address hazardous manual sanitation work. The discussion focused on understanding the specific task, the people involved and whether a simpler intervention could help. It was an idea being explored, not a completed deployment.

Inspection, finding a blockage and cleaning can require different solutions. A clear scope helps a team choose a manageable starting point and evaluate whether its work is useful.

A prototype is a limited version or representation of an idea made to answer a question. It might be a sketch, model, sample interaction or small demonstration. It need not be a finished product.

Speak with the people involved, verify the need, examine constraints and test a small version. Decide what evidence would justify continuing, changing direction or stopping.

Yes. Use it to prepare for real conversations rather than substitute for them.

TRY THIS PROMPT
I want to explore [problem] in [context]. Help me plan discovery interviews. List the people I should speak with, ten neutral questions and assumptions I need to test. Do not assume the proposed solution is correct. Suggest one small, low-cost way to learn more before building.

The marshmallow challenge

Build, test, listen & reflect

9 questions

Teams used spaghetti sticks, masking tape, thread and a marshmallow to build the tallest tower that could support the marshmallow at the top within twenty minutes. The discussion afterwards connected their choices to real projects.

It made planning, assumptions, testing and teamwork visible. These habits matter when applying technology as well as when building a physical structure.

They described a clear strategy, attention to the foundation and good communication. Their reflection offered useful lessons about how they worked together.

Its builders reflected on excitement, confidence and what they had overlooked. The exercise created a concrete opportunity to discuss how to test assumptions before a final attempt.

One participant had assumed the supplied thread was intended to confuse them. That interpretation affected their design. The discussion showed why it helps to make assumptions explicit and test them.

A useful reflection identifies what was tried, what happened and what should change next time. Sharing that reasoning can help the whole group learn without turning a mistake into a personal label.

Not necessarily. Listening includes understanding the suggestion and considering how it affects the next action. A polite acknowledgment without consideration can leave a useful idea unexplored.

Projects need clear goals, practical tests, communication and a foundation that supports later decisions. A striking design still needs to function when the team lets go.
